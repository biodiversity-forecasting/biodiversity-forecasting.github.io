Norway has a wealth of good knowledge about its species. But the data are unevenly distributed, fragmented and often not open – including data that are publicly funded and commissioned.

The information arena gathers open data on species and environment, corrects for biased sampling and delivers maps with uncertainty.

## From scattered observations to complete maps

Satellites, drones, fieldwork, environmental DNA and citizen science provide different kinds of data. Statistical models combine them into maps of where species are – and where we are uncertain.

The maps come from the [Hotspots projects at the NTNU Gjærevoll Centre](https://gjearevoll.github.io/hotspots-website/en/), which produce wall-to-wall maps of species diversity across Norway at 500 × 500 m resolution. You can explore the maps and the code there.

The work proceeds in five steps:

1. Open data on species and environment
2. Statistical modelling
3. Species-specific maps
4. Aggregated maps
5. Analyses against other data

## Where should we observe next?

The models point to where, when and for which species groups new data add the most value. Open data, open code and open results are the foundation of a verifiable basis for decisions.
